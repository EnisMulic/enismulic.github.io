The slibe.online timeline

5 September 2026·24 min read

This timeline has been created out of my own memory, the project’s git history, chat messages and conversations with other team member and external actors. It’s primary purpose is to serve as a recollection of the project details when I start forgetting them, as I already have.

On March 31st 2026 Bosnia & Herzegovina qualified to the FIFA World Cup for the second time in its history, the first time being in 2014, and just like 12 years ago, the Panini craze would be back again.

Stats

As of writing, slibe.online has been running for about 3 months. In that time, 12k+ users have registered for the app, 5.3+ million unique user-sticker combinations have been entered or 5.9+ million stickers when accounting for duplicates, almost 400k messages have been exchanged, and with a late introduction of the swap proposals, 32k+ swaps have been completed involving 358k+ stickers. Around 1600 users have marked their albums as completed, around 2000 have it at 90% completion, about 600 at 80%, followed by roughly 300 users in all categories from 70 to 10%, and finally about 1600 users are at the 0% mark. All in all, not too bad for a region-specific app dedicated to a small niche hobby.

April 8

On April 8, I ran into a post on /r/bih about how much money it would take to collect the upcoming Panini sticker album. A couple of days later I had talked to some of my friends from work, who I suspected would probably be collecting the stickers. On the same day, Omar and I rushed out of the office looking for stickers, we found none.

April 16–17

A week later, on April 16th I came to Abdulah with the idea of making an app to find swap partners. I had originally suggested the idea with the expectation that he would do it by himself. Knowing Abdulah wouldn’t start working on this immediately, and me having some free time that evening, I wrote the original specification file of all the features I wanted for the MVP version. By Friday morning I had a workable demo.

The initial version was very bare bones, the user could register and log in using their gmail, they would need to enter a “unique” username, or auto generate one, and could enter their contact information if they wanted, whether it be an email, social media, or phone number, and optionally they could enter their location as well. And most importantly, they could also manage their collection, what they had, needed, or had duplicates of, with the initial data set being placeholder codes and names such as the world class player labeled “Argentina - Player 15”.

April 18–19

Over the weekend I implemented the initial marketplace and logic for looking for swap partners and added some analytics so we could track how many people were using the app. I also found a blog that was leaking all of the important data we needed to make the app usable, like player names, sticker codes, and some of the initial low quality images used in the early stages of development.

April 20

On April 20th, I implemented the user chat (a feature that almost didn’t make the cut). I also implemented the initial swap initiation and confirmation flow, as well as a rating and reputation system to filter out any bad actors, a feature ABSOLUTELY NO ONE USED, probably because the UI/UX sucked. I also added a user’s public profile with some stats like number of stickers owned, missing, and completed trades (a feature ABSOLUTELY NO ONE USED). Additionally, Abdulah started working on the app and cleaned up the ui so it looks presentable on both desktop and mobile.

Originally I didn’t think about the name much when starting the project, I just picked a very obvious one-word Bosnian word, sličice (engl. stickers). When the time came to buy a domain name for the site, Abdulah suggested slibe, which I’ve learned recently is not Bosnia-wide but a Sarajevo-specific slang term for sličice, and so we went with that one because it sounded better name, agreed to not waste money on a .ba domain, and settled for the cheap .online one and shipped.

April 21

On April 21st, Faruk officially joined, and started working on a new layout for the album page. On the same day Abdulah implemented album sticker filters, added country flags in the sidebar and page headers, made the slibe icon, implemented the PWA version of the app, added a landing page, implemented chat push notifications. Meanwhile, I worked on the image handling for the stickers, the user reputation calculation system, the toggle to see sticker codes on the album page, and the translations for the application.

April 22–26

From April 22nd to 26th, we implemented marketplace filtering by city, added sticker codes to swap match details, and worked on translations and bug fixing to tighten up the app. I also came across an Instagram account (davepautac) that was posting images of all the player stickers, a slightly tedious resource as Dave was only posting players, ignoring emblems and team photos. He was also posting 1 or 2 teams per day, meaning we were slowly collecting the images, making our app look kind of half-finished. I found a site to scrape these images and upload them to a Supabase storage account. This would later cause our first major issue.

April 27–30

From April 27th to 30th, Faruk implemented the new two-column album layout that would become the album’s core look. Seeing his improvements, I played around with ordering the stickers in a layout that looked like the actual physical album, creating our app’s signature look.

April 30 – May 1

At this point, by the little marketing we did we gained around 170 users, now one thing I didn’t know about Supabase is that loading images from their CDN is super fucking expensive. With the few users we had and about 100 images, we’d already hit and passed the cached-egress quota of our free-tier account, and given how fast we’d hit that limit, upgrading to a $20/month plan wouldn’t have helped. The decision was simple: disable the images temporarily so our app wouldn’t crash or get deleted, and look for a different storage solution. We quickly came to Cloudflare’s R2, a service that charges a tiny fee for millions of reads, if you read the fine print, unlike me. We ended up having 14 million image requests at one point, so Cloudflare did end up costing us money, but probably less than if we had used Supabase’s solution.

Meanwhile, Faruk arranged a local sports-media page (“sportski radnik”) to feature us and share the app. This drove 50+ new signups in a single day, doubling my initial expectation of users and marking our 2nd milestone.

May 1–7

From May 1st to 7th, we implemented search using sticker codes, added a tutorial modal for first-time users, added an album completed state, added sticker label toggle persistence, and split FWC and FIFA museum stickers into their own sections.

On May 5th we got featured on vijesti.ba and on May 6th radiosarajevo.ba.

Sometime around this time Abdulah found an official Panini app (Panini Collectors) for tracking your stickers by scanning them. And one cool feature about the app is that you could share any sticker you scanned via message apps. Considering you needed to scan the sticker to “unlock” it, I figured you could probably use any picture of a player that was in the album to unlock it. So we googled “Edin Džeko”, scanned a random image and unlocked Džeko - confirming my suspicion - and we went to work. It took us a couple of weeks between the 3 of us (Zejd, Abdulah, and me) to scan all the teams and clean up the images for upload.

May 8–14

From May 8th to 14th, we shipped the HIFA Partnered Swap Events page, and we introduced “flat views” for missing and duplicate stickers. We added a view for displaying users who completed their albums but still had leftover stickers, and we also enabled users to hide themselves from the marketplace. As users grew, so did the costs of running the application, so we added a Ko-Fi donation link to try to offset some of these.

May 16–22

From May 16th to 22nd, we started working on automating swap requests and post-swap collection updates.

May 24–30

From May 24th to 30th, we added the extra sticker variants, causing our sticker table to go over 1000 rows and “breaking” the app. At the time we didn’t know that Supabase has a 1000-row read limit, so we had to implement a workaround to perform 2 queries in sequence so a user’s album could be loaded correctly.

We also removed Vercel Analytics as this was generating a lot of costs while providing almost no value to us. Alongside the Vercel Analytics, we decided to remove the collected section from the album page. On top of all that, we managed to finalize the swap requests and auto album updates.

May 27 – June 2

From May 27th to June 2nd, we added a page so users could report where they could buy stickers - a seemingly necessary feature considering the sticker scarcity and scalpers, at least here in Sarajevo.

I also implemented a CSV bulk import of stickers after seeing someone using the app and struggling to add his stickers one by one.

June 5–8

From June 5th to 8th, most of our efforts were spent on resolving frequent crashes we were facing by optimizing the matching algorithm as much as possible. We then added a debounce to the search filters to decrease calls to the database.

June 9–14

From June 9th to 14th, we made necessary changes so the users’ chat threads were ordered by last message sent, which was a nice UX improvement as well as a slight performance boost for the app. We introduced a query cache table to offload matching calculations that were being performed on every refresh. Alen sent us a redesign for the album and marketplace pages. While implementing the new design, I also took the opportunity to clean up the code base a bit, as it was becoming a huge mess. I decided to make the new design toggleable so the old users that had already developed a workflow within the app wouldn’t be put off by this giant change. Besides our Instagram account, we didn’t really have a system of promoting new features within the app, so the new design ended up being used by about 200 users.

June 17 – July 5

As you can see from the date range, things slowed down a bit in June and July. We didn’t do much: an occasional bug fix here and there, some performance enhancements, and small improvements that the users would ask for. The HIFA Events page finally got removed, and that’s pretty much it.

Reddit Marketing

One of our initial ideas was to market the app on the /r/bih subreddit, as we saw a good amount of people expressing interest in collecting the stickers; one problem though is that the subreddit does not allow self-promotion - a rule only applicable to non-administrators as we would find out. So we decided to make it a little bit “covert”: one account would post about collecting stickers in general, the other would comment the application URL, and we could maybe catch a couple of users this way.

None of us can remember the actual date or the throwaway accounts we used to do this. We can’t even remember if the post or comment was deleted - but what we can remember is that on the 4th of May the /r/bih moderators posted a thread promoting their own application.

At the time slibe.online was open source, with a link to the repository in the footer; we were convinced that they copied, reskinned the UI, and deployed our application. It certainly didn’t help that they also used a cheap .online domain. In hindsight, you can easily tell that the technology they used is way different than ours, so copying allegations are unlikely. They did, however, implement some things that we scrapped from our app. One thing they copied from us was the donation page, and looking at it, they were way behind us in every aspect. Although they were the first to make a counter app to ours, they were not the last.

Clone Wars

I mention that the Reddit application wasn’t the last “competitor” we had, and I don’t have enough to write about every individual “clone” that we found, so I’m just going to rapid-fire mention them in this section:

  • panini.ba - I found this one before the one from /r/bih, it had a counter on it when it would launch. It never did.
  • imamnemam.ba - Literally ripped images from our app and tried to copy our album layout. It didn’t really work out for him, and he shut the app down relatively soon after that.
  • olx.ba - Bosnian eBay basically had their own little section for trading. It looked really bad.
  • vijesti.ba - One of the first media outlets that promoted us. They used their own platform to try the whole swap thing - not sure if anyone used it. I didn’t even attempt to register to try it.
  • slicice.ba - This one had a 5BAM paywall to register, so I didn’t really want to test it. From their landing page, it seemed to have about 20ish users, so roughly 20-30BAM in profit, considering the domain name, and maybe a cheap server. The app was not running at the time of writing.
  • sli.ba - This one produced a pretty funny Facebook thread. To sum it up in a couple sentences, the app was made by a first-year CS student. He made it onto two different TV stations to promote his app, and when he posted it to the Facebook group for sticker trading, he got a bit of backlash from a couple of users that were already using our app for claiming to be the first trading app in Bosnia. I’d like to say that we are not affiliated with any of these people. We don’t know them personally, but somehow in the whole kerfuffle, we got called “College educated engineers”, which is pretty funny considering how little shit I give about my diploma, and the rest of the team is technically still in college.

HIFA Partnership

After the article on Radio Sarajevo, we got contacted by someone from HIFA Petrol’s marketing team for a potential collaboration. From the start I refused to be included in any of the correspondence with HIFA, so my recollection of events is a rough retelling from what I was told. The details of the deal were as follows: we would display a list of swap events organized at HIFA Petrol Station in our application, and in return they would promote the app on their social media sites, print promotional fliers in the good old year 2026, and try to involve their sponsors - and if there was any money to be made, we would also get paid. Well, we got most of these, not the app growth or money. We don’t even know if anyone showed up to these events. We ended up removing the events page maybe a bit too late. Unfortunately, no one signed any contracts, so they can deny any of this ever happening.

Getting on to Klix

On the 16th of May, klix.ba, Bosnia’s biggest news portal, posted an article about people gathering at the Historical Museum to swap stickers. As you open the article you were met with a video in which you can clearly see a person using slibe.online to track their collection, a small detail that would lead one of the reporters working on Klix to follow us on Instagram. Faruk messaged the guy about writing an article about the app, and got instructions to send our information to their marketing email. Two days later, Faruk got a call which resulted in the legendary line, “Is this Faruk from slibe.online?” On May 19th the article got published.

This would drive the biggest user growth so far. In a couple of days, we grew from 3k to 5k. At this point, we started to believe that 10k was achievable.

10k

In the early days, every user counted. Any free second we had, we were checking the numbers, and any minor jump, we would be reporting it in our chats. And as I’ve mentioned, my initial expectation was 100 users, max - we blew right past that. I can’t even remember when we got to 1,000, but we were probably screaming from joy. By the middle of May, we were supposed to be close to 3k; we hit and passed that milestone almost 7 days earlier than expected. Once Klix published the article about our app, we jumped over 5k in a couple of days, almost doubling our user base. As the numbers grew and we were achieving milestones previously thought impossible, we spent less time checking the users, at least until 10k was within reach. In the couple of days leading into our “final” milestone, we were checking like crazy, maybe even more than when we had 100 users. On June 17th, Croatia played England in the group stage, and I went out to watch the game. By the time I left my apartment, we were maybe a bit over 100 users away from the big number. Luckily, I couldn’t use my phone to check; otherwise, I would have been refreshing all game long and probably missed all 6 goals. After the game, I rushed back home so I wouldn’t miss the big number. When I got there, it was almost midnight; I don’t exactly remember how many users we were missing, maybe 5, maybe 10, but enough for me to stay up and refresh the Supabase dashboard until we got it. Luckily for me, we hit 10k on June 18th, 2026, at 00:15. We were all probably too excited, so no one remembered to screenshot this, meaning we don’t have any record of the event happening. Faruk even built a countdown tool to monitor the database every second so we wouldn’t miss it, but it doesn’t matter. I’ve worked in the software industry for 5 years now, building internal corporate apps that I’d be lucky if 100 users visited in a month, so building something that got this many people to sign up feels kind of cool, even though it lost me some money. After we got to our major milestone, we slowed down on the dashboard refreshing and would only occasionally check if someone asked where we were at. The signups also slowed down, with the app growing to about 12.5k in 3 months - not an insignificant number, but for us not comparable to the 10k we were so excited for. This would have been an amazing place to end this, but the surge of users, as amazing as it was, led to some major issues within the app that I will cover later in the blog.

Supabase Down

In order to not burn money, we ran this app on what felt like a toaster at times, a toaster that couldn’t keep up with the scale of growth. On top of that, the main functionality of matching users based on stickers was an AI-slop-generated black box. Even in the early days, the infrastructure would be overloaded by the end of the day, causing it to crash or perform horrendously. I’m not sure how many times we tried to “optimize” the algorithm, and I don’t want to check either, but in the many rewrites we attempted and arguments about how the displayed swap partners should be ordered, nothing seemed to be perfect. We added indexes - the data increased. We filtered out stickers to reduce the amount of calculations needed - the data increased again. We added a debounce to search filters, removed features that increased calls to the database, implemented query caches, and limited swap candidates. Yet again, the moment we got a user spike, we would be back to square one. As annoying as all of these were, they were fixable and manageable, unlike the series of unfortunate events that caused our underlying infrastructure service to crash globally, leaving all of our users unable to use the app. How do you fix a service you don’t own? You don’t. You just sit there and wait dumbfounded, reading all the angry messages from users as to why the app isn’t working. Supabase ended up fixing its issue after a couple of days, and we continued to work without any major crashes, as far as I can remember.

Things we wanted to do, but never got around to

The process of developing this app was pretty much chaos. Aside from a couple of the big features and a couple of things I had written down to implement myself, there was not much planning. We all kind of did our own thing, and the policy was: if you thought it was cool and wanted to make it or had the time, just do it. When you factor in that we did this in our free time, it means we didn’t implement everything we would have liked to. Later, when we get to the user feedback that we got, you may see some of these again, but without further ado, here is everything that we wanted to add:

  • A way to share missing and duplicate stickers with a link to the application itself - you might have seen another app do this, especially if you were in the BIH Panini Facebook group
  • A score predictor for the games themselves - we were going to have a little mini game with points and leaderboard for correctly guessing the winners and scores for each game. Abdulah did implement a proof of concept for the feature. Unfortunately, we started working on this way too late, and testing it would have meant that we could release it maybe halfway through the first round of the group stage, so we scrapped the idea
  • A way to delete chat threads
  • The Coca-Cola stickers were not ordered correctly, but some people already filled them out when we found this out, so we were too lazy to reorder them
  • We never found images for all of the extra stickers. I tried my best, checking Pinterest daily. I even tried recreating some of them with AI. That didn’t go so well for me.
  • A boost for people who were really close to completing the album - I wanted to implement something for people that were super close to completing their album, and could see everyone who had their missing stickers regardless of “swap compatibility” so they could negotiate, but this would have been a tricky margin to choose when to display this for the user.
  • We considered adding badges for completing certain activities in the app, like finishing a specific country, completing a certain amount of trades, etc. - This didn’t make the cut as the complexity of implementation did not out weight the value it would bring.

User Feedback

And before I end this with some parting words and credits, here is everything we learned from development, private messages, and the user submission form we ran 2 months before shutting down the app:

  • Using up-and-coming proprietary infrastructure cloud services, built on top of other proprietary infrastructure cloud services, creates a chain of dependency that will leave you stranded as soon as one of those explodes - tl;dr Supabase bad.
  • One of the quickest ways to grow your social media is by crashing your app to have a resurgence of angry customers flooding you with messages.
  • AI is pretty decent for scraping together an MVP in a couple of days; however, you might want to take the wheel as soon as possible.

As for the user feedback, here are some stats and issues our users were faced with that I’ll address: Note: we ran this when most people probably stopped using the app because they completed their albums or they didn’t like their experience, so the numbers will be skewed a bit.

37% of our users were between the ages of 35 and 44, 33% between 25 and 34, 16% were in the 18 to 24 range, 10% of users were younger than 18, and 4% were above the age of 55. A dominant 82% of our audience were male, 16% female, and 2% chose not to say. Only 11% of our users used the desktop version, while 89% of them used the mobile version. Word of mouth seems to be the best way to get people onto the app, as 58% answered that they found out about the app via friend recommendation, 14% found us via media like Klix and Radio Sarajevo, 12% Facebook, 9% Instagram (don’t buy ads on Instagram, kids), and 6% saw someone else using the app before choosing to sign up. Overall, 77% of users gave the app a rating of 5, 17% gave it a 4, and only 2 respondents gave it below 3. Most people who would have given it a 2 or a 1 weren’t using the app by the time we collected data. As for the usability, 66% of users gave a 5 for the first-use ease, adding/removing stickers ease got a 5 from 72% of users, while 22% of users said that they had moments of confusion while using the app. As per the survey, people seemed to really love the simplicity and ease of use, the swap-matching (or as they put it “who has what I need”), the visual album-like design, the PDF export, and the automatic collection updates after a trade (3 of these were not in the original launch). And now the bad things:

  • No notifications - we actually did have notifications, but you had to allow the web app to send them to you, so if you skipped this after signing up, it would be hard for non-technical users to figure it out.
  • No read receipts - never even crossed my mind to implement this, but I can see why it would be useful.
  • Navigation/pagination (can’t jump between teams, must page through album) - we did hear about this one before the survey, and we’re not sure how people missed the 3 lines in the top-left corner that would open the navigation bar with a list of sections/countries that would act as quick links. One thing I will admit is that we could have worded the search bar placeholder to point users to filter stickers via codes (e.g. MEX) instead of just the player names.
  • Many people complained that users were not responding to messages. Yeah, I can’t help you with that one - maybe try sending a thoughtful message. I don’t know.
  • Chat feature gaps like deleting messages and images - I did think about a delete-chat button, and deleting messages would probably be useful too. We were never going to do images, though; it would increase the cost of our infrastructure, and we were not willing to spend more money on a free-to-use app.
  • And probably most of the complaints were regarding the frequent app crashes, which I already addressed in a dedicated section.

Oh, and to the guy that said the nav bar didn’t make sense because there was too much scrolling and (imagine the most annoying nasally voice you can hear) “every good design start off with the least amount of scrolling, this could have been designed in a better way to avoid this”; FUCK YOU, but to the rest of you who filled out the form, thank you, your feedback will be considered for my next million-dollar app idea.

The End

Depending on when this gets published and when you are reading it, the app has been discontinued since September 1st, 2026. After the Ko-Fi donations, we lost about 150 BAM running this thing. Faruk completed Bosnia, Zejd completed his album, and Abdulah got close to completing his. He also got 10s in two different classes with this project and is working on his 3rd, hopefully. And I get this blog. Once again, thank you to everyone that used the application - hope we helped. Also, thank you to Faruk and Abdulah for jumping into development day one. Thank you, Zejd, for doing the marketing - I sure as hell wouldn’t have done that. Thanks to Alen for suggesting some of the later features and redesigning some of the pages in the second design of the app that we never fully launched. Thank you, Amina, for also consulting us on some of our design choices. Thank you, Samija, for covering a month of costs for Vercel. And thank you, Dženana (hvala, Dženana), for reviewing this blog!
